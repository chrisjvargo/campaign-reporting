Campaign Reporting:
one year of Subaru
on The Trade Desk

A full calendar year of real programmatic delivery: 1.48 billion impressions, $4.64 million, 1.12 million clicks across 47 Subaru campaigns, January–December 2018.

You already did the Excel mechanics on a slice of this workbook. Today is the grown-up version: turn a raw DSP export into a client report you'd defend in a room — scorecard, pacing, efficiency, caveats, and one decision.

Source workbook: `subaru.xlsx` (The Trade Desk export, 8 tabs). All figures in this deck are recomputed from row-level sums, weighted properly, with rollup rows removed. GTC is reported exactly as the workbook labels it — a recorded conversion signal, never "sales."

Speaker notes

Position: Social and Meta taught platform-native buying; this class teaches reporting on open-web programmatic where the export is messy and totals fight each other. Link back to Peter Brinkman's guest chat: this is the kind of delivery a trading desk produces, now students report it.

The job of a report

Five questions. Anything else is decoration.

1 · Delivery
Did it run? Where, when, how much?
→
2 · Audience & supply
Who and what did we actually buy?
→
3 · Response
Clicks, viewability, recorded conversions
→
4 · Efficiency
What did each outcome cost, vs. what?
→
5 · Decision
What changes next flight?

A weak report dumps every tab of the export into slides. A strong one picks a base, reconciles it, disaggregates on purpose, and puts the caveats where the client can see them.

The tell of a weak report

Averages of ratios (a "mean CTR" across unequal rows), percentages with no counts, totals summed across tabs that describe the same impressions eight times. All three mistakes are sitting inside this workbook, waiting. We will make none of them.

Speaker notes

Recall the earlier Subaru exercise rules: weighted CTR from totals, floors before interpretation, blank-hour rollups out. Today generalizes those rules to a full reporting workflow.

Every ratio in this deck is built the same way: sum the numerator, sum the denominator, then divide. The workbook's own CTR/CPM columns are row-level ratios — averaging them weights a 12-impression row the same as a 22-million-impression row. We never do that.

Name things honestly

15.7 million "conversions." For cars. At $0.30 each.

"If GTC were car sales, Subaru would have sold fifteen million cars in these markets in 2018. It did not."

What GTC can be

  • A recorded post-impression signal in the DSP export (the workbook's Conversion Rate: GTC (Imps) ≈ 1.06% of impressions).
  • 14× the click count (15,700,091 vs. 1,124,693) — so most GTC arrives without a click on that impression: view-through windows, cross-device paths, or a very generous pixel.
  • Useful as a relative signal across cuts, with the definition attached.

What GTC is not

  • Not vehicle sales, not dealer visits, not proven lift. No holdout lives in this workbook.
  • Not comparable to clicks as a funnel step — you cannot "convert" 14 times per click.
The sentence to use

"The export records 15.7M GTC events ($0.30 each at base). We treat GTC as a directional recorded signal, not sales, and we never report it without that label." Instant deduction in your assignment for calling GTC "conversions" or "sales" with no qualifier.

Speaker notes

This mirrors GOAL.md's standing rule for this data: preserve reported rates, never describe recorded conversions as proven sales or causal lift. PHL New Car (huge GTC, few clicks) is the in-workbook proof that GTC ≠ click-path conversions.

Pacing · full year

Front-loaded year: February spent like it meant it

Bars: monthly cost (base tab). Red line: weighted CTR by month. Feb–Mar is the spend peak ($646k / $632k); May troughs at $277k; December rebounds to $396k with the year's second-best click month (116,100 clicks).

Report pacing like this

Spend share by month vs. outcome share by month. March records the most GTC (2.16M) on near-peak spend; October records the least (742k) on the lowest spend — flag both, explain neither beyond the evidence without flight briefs, incentives, or inventory data we don't have.

CPM drift is the quiet story

CPM slides from $3.37 (Feb) to $2.85 (Nov) across the year. Same dollars bought ~18% more impressions in November than in February. Cheap reach or degrading supply? The format/supply cuts answer that — pacing alone cannot.

Speaker notes

No budget/flighting brief in workbook — teach restraint: describe shape, ask what external calendar (model-year launches, incentive months) would explain it, don't invent causality. CTR dips Jul–Aug (0.060–0.062%) then recovers.

Daypart & weekday

Evenings click and record. Overnights only click.

Bars: impressions by user hour. Red line: weighted CTR. CTR peaks 19:00–23:00 (0.089–0.097%) where cost per GTC is also cheapest ($0.21–$0.27). Overnight CTR looks fine (0.083–0.089% at 01:00–04:00) — until cost per GTC triples ($0.57–$0.79).

Weekday is boring, and boring is information: Mon–Thu CTR 0.073–0.074%, weekend 0.080–0.081% with slightly fewer recorded GTC. No DOW story strong enough to re-flight around; the hour story is strong enough to test.

The reporting move

Always pair the action metric with the outcome metric before recommending a daypart shift. "Overnight CTR is high" and "overnight is where cheap recorded outcomes live" are opposite claims in this data. You saw the click-vs-GTC tension in the exercise; at full-year scale it survives every floor we apply.

Speaker notes

Blank-hour rollup rows excluded by construction (dimension group excludes nulls). Test design to propose: evening-weighted cell vs. control at equal CPM caps, judged on cost per GTC and a downstream check the client trusts — not GTC alone.

Never report one column of this table

Device strategy from CTR alone kills PC. Device strategy from GTC alone kills tablet. The honest report keeps both columns, names the attribution window problem, and asks for a downstream truth (dealer-site sessions, leads) before reallocating.

This is why reporting starts with floors: our campaign reads use meaningful-delivery lines only, and every rate in your assignment needs its denominators printed next to it.

Floor rules for your report

State your floor (e.g., ≥1M impressions or ≥$10k per line), apply it before ranking anything, and keep a one-line "excluded as immaterial" note. A floor you declare is method. A floor you hide is cherry-picking.

Speaker notes

Ghost GTC totals (~256k across these rows) also contaminate base GTC slightly — another reason the headline reports GTC with its label. Exercise used a 100-impression floor; full-report floors should be far stricter at this scale.

The counterfactual, DSP edition

What would Subaru have sold with zero ads?

Why this export can't answer that

  • No holdout, no geo split, no exposed/unexposed contrast — everyone in the file saw ads.
  • Conquesting targets rival shoppers because they're in-market; retargeting-style lines harvest demand that was already coming.
  • GTC's window and rules aren't documented in the workbook. A metric with no stated window gets reported with a question mark, not a victory lap.

What you'd ask the desk for

  • TTD conversion settings: window, view-through share, dedup vs. search/social claims on the same shopper.
  • A lift design for next flight: geo holdouts on matched dealer markets, or audience-split cells at equal bids.
  • Downstream truth: dealer-site sessions/leads by market-week to sanity-check GTC geography (does PHL really behave like that?).
Our verdict, honestly

This workbook proves delivery, response, and relative efficiency at massive scale. It does not prove the ads caused a single additional car sale. Report the first; never smuggle in the second.

The deliverable

Anatomy of a report I'd sign

HEADLINE (one sentence, denominators attached)
"In 2018, Subaru spent $4.64M for 1.48B Trade Desk impressions across 47 market campaigns (CPM $3.14), earning 1.12M clicks (weighted CTR 0.076%, CPC $4.13) and 15.7M recorded GTC events — a directional signal, not sales."
RECONCILIATIONSCORECARDCUTS
Base tab named, rollups removed, tab disagreements disclosed. Campaign scorecard by market/tactic. Two or three purposeful cuts (pacing, daypart, format, device, supply) — each with click and outcome columns.
CAVEATS ON THE PAGE
GTC labeled, window unknown. Floors declared, ghosts excluded by name. Partial-coverage tabs flagged. No causal verbs without a design.
DECISION + TEST
"Shift-test 15% of 300x250/160x600 budget into evening-weighted 320x50-class delivery in two matched markets; judge on CPC, cost per GTC, and dealer-site sessions — then decide." A report that changes nothing was a diary.

Guardrails, laminated

  • One base. Never sum tabs. Reconcile before you report.
  • Weighted ratios only: sum, then divide. Print denominators.
  • Declare floors; name excluded ghosts and garbage rows.
  • Click winner ≠ outcome winner — show both, every cut.
  • Cheap outcomes get audited before they get budget.
  • Unknown is a finding (37% fold-unknown; 722M tail impressions), not a rounding error.

Your turn · 5 points

Write the full-year Subaru report

One page plus one exhibit page. You're the analyst; the dealer-group CMO has read too many dashboards.

Your report must have

  • Headline + reconciliation paragraph (base, rollups, floors — by the numbers).

Instant deductions

  • Summing tabs, averaging the CTR column, or ranking ghost campaigns.

  • Any % with no counts; any recommendation with no test.
